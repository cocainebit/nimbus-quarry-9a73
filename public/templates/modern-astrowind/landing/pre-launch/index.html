What is coming

Tell them what, when and why now

A coming-soon page has three facts to deliver. Each card below is one, and says how to state it.

What: the product in one sentence

Name the outcome, not the category. “Publish a fast site in an afternoon” beats “a modern web platform”. If you cannot say it in one line yet, the page is early.

When: a date or an honest window

A real date if you have one, a window if you do not. Never a countdown you might reset. The roadmap below shows how to make a window feel concrete.

Why now: what early signups get

Early access, a launch price, a say in the roadmap. The reason to sign up today rather than after launch is the whole conversion argument of this page.

FAQ

Questions people ask before joining a waitlist

Short answers. The same answers feed a FAQ rich result in search, which is how a coming-soon page gets found before launch.

What is a pre-launch or coming soon landing page?

A page published before the product is available. It explains what is coming and collects email addresses from people who want to know when it does. Its success metric is signups, not sales.

What should I offer in exchange for the email?

Something only early signups get: early access, a launch discount, a founder price, or simply being first in line. Say it in the form’s title, not in the fine print.

Do I need a countdown?

Only if the date is real and near. A countdown to an unknown date, or one that gets reset, destroys trust. A launch window (“early next quarter”) with a roadmap is more honest and works just as well.

How much of the product should I show?

Something real: a screenshot, a short clip, a prototype. A page with only adjectives asks people to trust a promise; a page with one real image asks them to trust their eyes.

What do I send to the people who signed up?

A confirmation right away, then two or three short updates before launch (progress, a behind-the-scenes look, the exact date). Silence between signup and launch is where waitlists die.
